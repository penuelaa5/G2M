# Competitive Intelligence & Strategic Bet · Roamly Groups

## 1. Primary competitor

- **Competitor:** Wanderlog
- **Core offering:** Tech-savvy 25-35 friend groups planning trips together. Wanderlog is one of the most-used collaborative trip planners in that cohort; Troupe plays a similar game on the group-decision side (polling dates and destinations).
- **How they position themselves:** As “the most widely used map-based trip planner.” The pitch is visual organization: put your trip on a map, drag stops around, cluster things by neighborhood so you’re not crisscrossing a city. It’s positioned as the place to organize a trip you’ve already roughly figured out, with collaborative editing and map sharing built in. Not a booking engine, not a recommender. A shared canvas.
- **Primary audience:** Groups who all edit the same trip, people who already have a rough plan and want to see it on a map, and travelers who import their Google Maps saved lists or Gmail booking confirmations. It skews tech-comfortable and group-oriented, which is the same 25-35 friend-group cohort you’re chasing. “Large groups where everyone edits the same map” is called out as a core use case.
- **Known pricing or packaging:** Free: $0, and it’s genuinely generous. Unlimited collaborators, full map-based planning, manual budget tracking, and basic expense splitting. AI features are capped per trip.
Pro: $39.99/year, or $16.99/month if you pay monthly. Pro adds route optimization, unlimited attachments, and more AI interactions.
- **Why they're the biggest threat:** First, they already own the behavior you’re trying to introduce. Your organizer may already have the trip living in Wanderlog before Roamly ever enters the conversation. You’re not teaching a new habit, you’re trying to pry an existing one loose.

Second, and this is the finding that matters: they ship crude versions of two of your three pillars. Shared itinerary is their core strength. Expense splitting already exists, described as basic and manual. So your differentiation isn’t “we do itineraries and splitting and they don’t.” It’s narrower than that. It’s “we make the itinerary actually bookable, with curated local inventory and real split payments, in one place.” That’s a real wedge, but it’s a thinner one than your value prop currently implies, and your team should know the gap is smaller than it looks.

Third, the attack direction favors them. It is far easier for a free planning tool to add a “book this” button than for a booking marketplace to earn the daily planning habit. If Wanderlog ever bolts payments and inventory onto the itinerary people already build there, they come straight at your core. You’re trying to move upstream into planning from booking; they’re one feature release from moving downstream into booking from planning, and they start with the engaged user.

## 2. Battlecard

### Competitive Battlecard — Wanderlog

**Roamly Groups vs. Wanderlog** · Internal — draft · Nov 4, 2026

> **The one line:** Wanderlog plans the trip. Roamly makes it happen — book curated local experiences and split the cost without anyone leaving the group or fronting the bill.

---

#### 1. Know the Landscape

##### Competitor overview
Wanderlog is the most widely used **map-based collaborative trip planner**. It's a visual organizer: drop stops on a map, cluster by neighborhood, drag to reorder, and let the whole group edit the same trip. It is **not** a booking engine and holds no inventory. It helps groups *plan* a trip, not transact one.

##### Key offerings
- **Shared map itinerary** — real-time collaborative editing, unlimited collaborators
- **Route optimization** and neighborhood clustering
- **Imports** from Google Maps lists and Gmail booking confirmations
- **Basic expense splitting** and manual budget tracking
- **AI planning** (capped on the free tier)

##### Pricing
| Tier | Price | What's included |
|---|---|---|
| Free | $0 | Generous: maps, unlimited collaborators, basic splitting |
| Pro | $39.99/yr (or $16.99/mo) | Adds route optimization, unlimited attachments, more AI |

**Note:** Group collaboration lives in the *free* tier. Wanderlog monetizes power features, not the group behavior itself.

---

#### 2. Understand the Battlefield

##### Their key strengths
- **Owns the planning habit.** The organizer may already have the trip in Wanderlog before Roamly enters the room.
- **Free and genuinely good.** No price objection to overcome.
- **Best-in-class maps** and visual group editing.
- **Already does 2 of our 3 pillars** — shared itinerary (strong) and splitting (basic).
- **Frictionless group adoption** — unlimited free collaborators; no one pays to join.

##### Their key weaknesses
- **Can't book anything.** No inventory, no transaction. You still leave for five other tabs.
- **No curated local experts** — pins on a map, not vetted experiences.
- **Money doesn't move.** Splitting tracks who owes whom; someone still fronts the bill.
- **No revenue relationship** with the traveler at the moment of purchase.

---

#### 3. Win the Deal

##### Why Roamly wins
- **Plan → book → pay in one loop.** The itinerary is actually bookable; nobody leaves the group to transact.
- **Curated, vetted local experts** — real inventory across 40 cities, not map pins.
- **Real split payments.** Money actually moves; no one fronts the deposit.

**Why it's defensible:** this requires a two-sided marketplace of vetted supply plus payments infrastructure. A planning tool can't stand that up quickly — it's a different company.

##### Why they win (say it honestly)
- **If the job is just planning**, they're free and better at it than we are.
- **Our itinerary and splitting aren't differentiators** — they already have both, good enough.
- **The habit is theirs.** Switching cost is social: the organizer must move four friends.
- **Attack direction favors them** — easier to add a "book" button than to earn the planning habit.

##### Why you (the one line)
Wanderlog plans the trip. Roamly makes it happen: book curated local experiences and split the cost without anyone leaving the group or fronting the bill.

---

#### 4. Handle the Hard Moments

##### Quick dismiss
> "Wanderlog is a beautiful notebook. It can't book a thing and no real money moves — so you still end up on five other tabs and someone still fronts the deposit. We close that loop."

##### Landmine questions
*Plant these; Wanderlog can't answer them well.*
- "When you've got the plan, **can you actually book and pay the experts right there** — or do you jump to other sites?"
- "When you split a cost, **does the money actually move**, or is it just tracking who owes whom?"
- "Are those experiences **vetted**, or just places someone pinned on a map?"
- "Who's stuck **fronting the deposit** for the group, and how do they get paid back?"

##### Objection handling
*Don't fight their strength — reframe the job.*

**"Wanderlog is free and we already use it."**
Keep planning there if you love it — it's great at that. The free part ends the second you need to book and pay. That's where the organizer becomes the group's bank. Roamly picks up exactly there: curated experiences, booked and split, money actually moving.

**"Wanderlog already does expense splitting."**
It tracks a tab — it tells you Dave owes $80. It doesn't move the $80 and it can't book the thing Dave owes for. We're the difference between a spreadsheet of IOUs and the trip actually being paid for.

**"Why would we add another app to the mix?"**
Fair — the goal is fewer apps, not more. Today the "stack" is a planner plus a payment app plus five booking tabs plus one friend doing all the work. Roamly collapses the booking-and-paying half into one place. Net apps go down, not up.

**"What if Wanderlog just adds booking?"**
Real risk, worth naming. But booking isn't a button — it's vetted two-sided supply across 40 cities plus payments and payouts. That's the hard part, and it's a head start they'd have to build from zero. Our job is to be clearly ahead on it before they try.

## 4. Strategic bet

### Data
- **Who Roamly Groups is for:** High-income tech professionals aged 25-35 in hubs like the Bay Area who travel abroad in friend groups and already automate friction out of their lives. More precisely, it’s for one person in each of those groups: the organizer who picks the dates, fronts the deposit, and chases everyone to pay them back.
- **The one thing worth choosing it for:** Roamly closes the loop. It’s the only option that turns a group itinerary into booked, paid, curated experiences in one place, with real money actually moving. Everyone else stops at tracking who owes whom.
- **The biggest threat to that position:** Wanderlog. It owns the planning habit for free and already ships shared itineraries and basic splitting. It sits one “book” button away from flanking Roamly from the planning side, and it starts with the engaged user we’re trying to win.

### Insight
The signal that changes how I think about the market
The behavioral one: people keep coming back as individuals and build manual workarounds rather than leave. That tells me the demand isn’t for a better planner, it’s for a transaction the current product won’t let them finish. The problem lives at the booking-and-payment layer, not the planning layer.
What the competitor’s win tells me to do differently
Wanderlog wins by owning the free, daily planning habit. The lesson is not to fight them there. Roamly shouldn’t try to be a better planner. It has to win the transaction, making booking and splitting so good that the plan gets pulled into Roamly, instead of trying to drag the planning habit away from a free tool people already love.

### Belief
I now believe that Roamly Groups wins when it closes the plan-to-booked-and-paid loop for the group’s organizer, because the real pain is completing a group transaction, not planning the trip, and no competitor actually completes it.

### Bet
- **I am betting that Roamly Groups wins by:** I am betting that Roamly Groups wins by owning the group transaction: making it effortless for one organizer to get their friends booked, paid up, and committed in one place, so the product spreads group by group through the people who already do the coordinating.
- **To make this bet, I am deprioritizing:** To make this bet, I am deprioritizing planning-tool parity with Wanderlog, the multi-geography rollout, the local-expert payments marketplace, and every segment except the high-income friend-group organizer.
