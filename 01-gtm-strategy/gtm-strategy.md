## 5. V1 GTM strategy one-pager

### Roamly Groups — V1 GTM strategy (draft)

**The bet:** Win high-income Bay Area tech professionals aged 25-35 who travel abroad in friend groups, by killing coordination friction so cleanly that the group's natural organizer adopts Roamly Groups and drags the rest of the group onto Roamly to book. Acquire first. Monetization is the next question, not this one.

#### Refined strategic inputs

Four inputs got sharpened from the original Discover answers. These are the versions that hold up.

| Input | Original | Sharpened |
|---|---|---|
| Segment | 25-35 friend travelers | High-income Bay Area tech professionals, 25-35, who already travel abroad in friend groups and want to automate the friction out of it |
| Value prop | "Aggregated, interconnected visibility of booking, payments, travel in one click" | Free the person who always ends up organizing the group trip. Less a dashboard, more a weight off one specific person's shoulders |
| ARPU mechanism | "Expand ARPU by 20%"  3 months after release | Resolve before spend: does a 5-person group trip earn Roamly **more margin** than 5 solo bookings, or the **same margin, stickier**? The GTM is the same either way; the business case is not |
| Competition | Splitwise, Airbnb Experiences, Google Sheets | The real rival is a group chat + a shared spreadsheet + one organized friend. It is free and already installed. You beat it by doing what it structurally cannot: actual booking and inventory, not just tracking |

The segment swap is the important one. It moves the target from lowest willingness-to-pay to a group that has money, acute coordination pain, and a persona (the engineer who automates everything) that maps directly to the adoption driver.

#### Target segment and the organizer

**Who:** Bay Area tech professionals, 25-35, high disposable income, comfortable with tools like Splitwise and Notion, who already travel abroad with friends. First destinations: Europe and Asia. These are current non-users of Roamly who travel and like testing new places.

**Why this segment wins:** They have money (willingness to pay is real, not aspirational), they feel the coordination pain acutely because they travel in groups often, and their instinct is to automate annoying manual work. That instinct is the whole adoption thesis.

**The unit of acquisition is not a user. It is a group.** And every group has one person who does the work: picks the dates, builds the spreadsheet, fronts the deposit, chases everyone to pay them back. Call them the organizer. In this segment, the organizer is usually the most tool-fluent person in the group, which is exactly who you are selling to.

That reframes the entire motion. You are not acquiring five people. You are acquiring one motivated organizer who recruits the other four. If the product does not give the organizer a reason to pull their group in, and an easy way to do it, the growth loop never fires. Everything in the channel and metric sections below is built around that one person.

#### Value proposition

**For the friend who always ends up running the group trip:** Roamly Groups does the annoying parts for you. One place to see who's in, who's paid, and what's booked, so you stop being the group's accountant and travel agent. Everyone books their own piece. Nobody owes you money at the end.

That is the message. It leads with the person and the relief, not the architecture. The features (shared itinerary, split payments, permissions) are how it works, not why anyone switches.

One discipline note: do not promise "one click" in the marketing. Group booking with split payments across separate accounts is genuinely hard, and if the ad says one click while the product ships as four-plus-a-Venmo, marketing is writing a check the product can't cash. Promise the outcome (no chasing, no fronting money), not a click count.

#### Channel approach

PLG is right, but "PLG" is not a channel. The product spreads inside a group that hasn't adopted it yet, so the channel work is about getting organizers in and making the invite effortless. Three layers, in priority order.

**1. The in-product viral loop (the real engine).** When an organizer creates a group, inviting the other four is the core action, not an afterthought. The invite has to work for someone with zero Roamly account: open a link, see the trip, pay their share, no forced signup wall before they see value. Every invited member who travels is a warm lead who has now experienced the product. This loop is the whole motion. If it works, paid acquisition just feeds it. If it doesn't, nothing else matters.

**2. Seed the organizers directly.** You know exactly who they are and where they are (Bay Area, tech, high income). Reach them where they already plan trips: targeted founder/engineer communities, group-travel and digital-nomad subreddits and Discords, and warm referral incentives for organizers who bring a full group. Content that speaks to the organizer's specific pain ("stop being your friend group's Venmo") will outperform generic travel ads.

**3. Paid, but only to pour fuel on a loop that already burns.** Hold paid acquisition until the viral loop shows a group invite-acceptance rate worth amplifying. Running paid before the loop converts just buys expensive signups that stall. Target the organizer persona, not the group.

What this is **not**: a sales motion. No demos, no negotiation, D2C all the way, consistent with the self-serve instincts of the target segment.

#### Success metric

Your goal is Acquire, so the North Star has to measure whether the acquisition loop fires, not whether people pay yet. Free-to-pay is a monetization metric and it belongs as a guardrail, not the headline. Here's the split.

**North Star: group activation rate.** The share of created groups where at least 3 members complete a real booking. This is the one number that proves the whole thesis, because it requires an organizer to start a group AND successfully pull their friends in AND those friends to book. One metric, testing the entire loop. A created group that never gets its members to book is a failed loop, and this metric catches it where a signup count would hide it.

**Time to value as the leading indicator** (your instinct here was right). Measure time from group creation to first completed group booking. Short and getting shorter means the friction promise is real. This is your early-warning gauge weeks before activation data matures.

**Guardrails (watch, don't optimize for, in v1):**

1. High signups without group activation. Your own flagged warning sign. It means you're attracting curiosity but the loop stalls. This is the metric that tells you to stop paid spend.
2. Invite acceptance rate. The organizer invites four; how many show up and book? This is the viral coefficient in disguise and it decides whether PLG compounds or leaks.
3. Free-to-pay. Hold it as a Phase 2 North Star. Watch it in v1 for signal, but do not let it distract an acquisition-stage launch into premature monetization.

One line to put on the wall: **a signup is not acquisition. An activated group is.**

#### Cut from v1, and open questions

**Cut from v1:**

1. Multi-geography targeting (US vs EU vs LATAM traveler behavior). You committed to Bay Area organizers traveling to Europe and Asia. Ship that. The three-way geographic comparison is a Phase 2 problem; carrying it into v1 just multiplies complexity.
2. The local-expert payment angle. It's a genuinely different business (two-sided marketplace, supply-side WTP, payment infrastructure) smuggled into a consumer PLG launch. Interesting, real, and parked.
3. The five-segment list. You've made the bet. Retire the other four from the launch plan so the team isn't hedging.

**Open questions to resolve over the coming weeks (in priority order):**

1. **The ARPU mechanism.** More margin per group trip, or same margin but stickier? Write the unit economics on one line before any spend. Everything downstream depends on it.
2. **Is it a painkiller or a vitamin?** When a group trip falls apart, is it because splitting payments was annoying (you fix that) or because someone flaked and the group couldn't agree on budget (you don't)? If it's the social/decision layer, this feature is a vitamin. Validate with real group-trip post-mortems from the target segment.
3. **The 3x repeat-booking signal.** Is it real data or aspiration? If real, it's your single strongest asset and should headline every pitch. If aspirational, stop calling it a signal.
4. **The organizer recruitment loop.** Why does the organizer get their four friends to switch from the free group-chat-plus-spreadsheet stack they already use? That's the load-bearing wall of the whole PLG motion. If there's no crisp answer, the loop won't fire.

This is a first draft. The segment and motion are sound. Questions 1 and 2 are the ones that could change the strategy, so chase those first.
